Factory floor: four 3D directions

The current 3D floor looks flat because the camera faces the grid straight on. Each concept below puts the same floor (eight work lines, every station, every count) under a different camera, metaphor and motion. Drag to turn each one, use the buttons to tilt, and hover, tap or tab to a station to read it. On phones, concepts 1 to 3 turn the floor a quarter so the lines run down the screen, and station names move into the readout under the view.

1

Isometric conveyor floor

Fixed angled camera, no perspective · lines are belts · stations are machines

Why

The floor as seen from a mezzanine above the plant. Each work line is a conveyor belt and each station a machine sitting on it. The stack of white blocks on a machine is the number of items waiting there, so busier stations stand taller. A machine that needs a person turns orange and lights a lamp. The camera is orthographic: there is no perspective, so every belt is the same length on screen and stack heights compare honestly, front to back.

A Lead reads at a glance

  • Where work piles up: the tallest stacks.
  • Where a person is needed: the orange machines with lamps.
  • Which way work flows: boxes riding the belts left to right.

Pros

  • Most literal factory story; motion shows flow.
  • No shrinking at the back, so labels stay one size.

Cons

  • On phones the belts turn to run down the screen and only counts fit; names show on tap.
  • Tall front stacks can hide stations behind them when tilted low.
  • Moving belts are busy on a screen people read for a long time.
2

Tabletop city

Perspective camera on a slow orbit · lines are streets · stations are buildings

Why

The floor as a model city on a table. Each line is a street; each station is a building along it, one storey per work item, and empty stations are bare lots. A station that needs a person is an orange building with a lit beacon on its roof and a ring pulsing on the ground, visible from any angle. Agents walk the pavements to the stations they are working at. The camera drifts slowly around the table so the skyline reads in depth.

A Lead reads at a glance

  • The skyline: where work has piled up.
  • Beacons: where people are needed.
  • Quiet streets: lines that are clear.

Pros

  • Strongest "spot the pile from across the room" view; suits a wall screen.
  • Beacons stay visible over tall buildings.

Cons

  • Perspective makes back streets smaller.
  • The view keeps moving, so the mental map shifts.
  • A city says less about order and flow than a line does.
3

Tilted swimlane board

Board leaned back like a drafting table · cards lift on hover · agents slide between stations

Why

The approved flat floor, picked up and leaned back on a table. Lines stay as rows top to bottom and stations as cards left to right, so switching between Flat and 3D loses nothing. Work items are dark tiles stacked on each card like a small deck. The board leans gently toward your pointer, and the card you point at lifts off the board. Tola, Chidi and Zainab slide from card to card as they move between the stations they work at.

A Lead reads at a glance

  • The same reading order as the flat board.
  • Orange cards: where people are needed.
  • Thick decks: where work piles up; agents show where work is moving now.

Pros

  • Easiest to read and closest to the approved board.
  • Works on a phone; the calmest motion.

Cons

  • The least "factory" of the four.
  • Depth adds feel more than new information.
4

Stepped amphitheatre

Low front camera · lines are curved tiers rising away from you · agents leave a coordinator desk

Why

The floor as a stepped stage seen from the front row. Each line is a curved tier, and every tier sits one step higher than the one in front, so no line hides another and the camera can stay low and dramatic. Payroll is nearest; Pre-sale is the top tier. Back tiers are wider, which cancels out most of the perspective shrink, so labels stay a similar size. At the front sits the coordinator desk: Tola, Chidi and Zainab leave it, climb to the station they are working at, and come back, while orange columns mark where people must decide.

A Lead reads at a glance

  • Every line at once, with nothing hidden behind anything else.
  • Orange columns: where people are needed.
  • Which agent is out on which line right now.

Pros

  • The most depth with no hidden stations.
  • Shows the coordinator handing work to agents.

Cons

  • Line order is front-to-back, not top-to-bottom as on the flat board.
  • The fan shape is wide, so on phones it narrows and stations sit closer.
  • Climbing agents add motion near the front.

How to choose

  1. The floor must read like the flat board and stay calm on a working screen: choose 3.
  2. The factory story matters most (belts, machines, visible flow): choose 1.
  3. It will mostly run on a wall screen to spot piles from across the room: choose 2.
  4. You want real depth with nothing hidden, and to see agents being sent out from the coordinator: choose 4.

Parts can be mixed, for example the board from 3 with the lamps from 1, or the coordinator desk from 4 on any of the others. Every concept uses the same data, labels, highlight and reduced-motion rules, so the choice is about the picture, not the facts.
